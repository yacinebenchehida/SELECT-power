SPOT: Selection Power Over Time

Estimates the power to detect selection at one candidate locus from allele frequencies sampled at two or more points in time. It accounts for genetic drift, haploid, diploid or haplodiploid inheritance, changes in population size, which sex is sampled, related individuals in the sample, and genotype calls from low-coverage sequencing. Every parameter change re-runs fresh replicates under selection and under neutrality, in the browser, typically in well under a second.

Model: one biallelic autosomal locus, discrete generations, separate numbers of breeding females and males (or a single haploid population), and selection that can differ between the sexes. Power is estimated by Monte Carlo against a drift-aware null. The README explains every parameter.

What "power" means here

Power is the probability that a sampling design detects selection when selection is actually acting. A power of 80% means that selection of the chosen strength is detected in 8 cases out of 10 and missed in 2.

  1. Replay history many times. The simulator replays the population's past on the computer: by default 1,000 times with the allele favoured by selection, and 2,000 times with no selection at all (more when many loci are tested).
  2. Sample and sequence. In every replay, specimens are drawn at each sampling date and "sequenced" with the same limits as real data: a limited number of specimens per date, and some genotypes read wrongly or not at all.
  3. Score each replay. One number per replay says how clearly the allele became more common over time. A high score means a clear rise.
  4. Set the threshold with the no-selection replays. Even without selection, allele frequencies wander up and down by chance (drift), a lot in small populations. The threshold is set so that only 5% of these chance-only replays clear it (5% is the significance level, adjustable in the settings).
  5. Count. Power is the share of the selection replays that clear the threshold.

Why the threshold matters: a standard test that ignores drift sets the threshold too low, so it often "finds" selection where there is none. The tile "False-positive rate of a naive test" shows how often that happens with the current settings. If power stays low whatever the sample size, chance drift is the limit, not the number of specimens: the sample-size planner below shows this.

Method

How power is computed: technical details

Analytic baseline (deterministic trajectory)

A common back-of-envelope calculation. The allele frequency follows a deterministic path from p₀ to pt, and the only noise is binomial sampling of n₁ alleles at the start and n₂ at the end. Power is the probability, summed over every possible pair of sample counts, that the test rejects:

Power = Σₓ Σᵧ Bin(x; n₁, p₀) · Bin(y; n₂, pₜ) · 1[ test(x, y) rejects at α ]

The threshold comes from sampling noise alone, so drift is assumed away. The "Analytic baseline" preset reproduces this setting.

This simulator: Monte Carlo with a drift-aware null

  1. Simulate R replicate populations under selection and 2R without selection (more when many loci are tested, so that about 10 neutral replicates fall beyond the threshold). Both start at the same p₀ and have the same breeding numbers, so drift acts on both.
  2. At each sampling point, draw the chosen number of individuals. Females, and diploid males, carry two alleles. Haploid males carry one.
  3. Pass each individual through sequencing: depth ~ Poisson(mean), alt reads ~ Binomial(depth, expected allele fraction). Call the genotype by maximum likelihood. Individuals below the minimum depth are dropped.
  4. Compute the test statistic Z for each replicate from the called allele counts.
  5. Set the drift-aware threshold at the 1 − α/m quantile of the neutral Z values.
  6. Power is the fraction of replicates under selection whose Z exceeds that threshold. The naive test's false-positive rate is the fraction of neutral replicates that exceed z₁₋α/m.

The test statistic

Both statistics are the Cochran–Armitage trend test on allele counts. At sampling point i (time ti in generations), xi copies of the selected allele are called out of ni alleles:

Z = Σᵢ xᵢ (tᵢ − t̄) / √( p̄(1 − p̄) Σᵢ nᵢ (tᵢ − t̄)² ) t̄ = Σ nᵢtᵢ / Σ nᵢ , p̄ = Σ xᵢ / Σ nᵢ

The trend version uses all sampling points. The two-point version uses only the first and last points, which reduces to a one-sided two-proportion z-test. Both are one-sided, testing for an increase of the selected allele. With sampling noise alone, Z ~ N(0, 1). Drift adds variance between generations, which widens the real neutral distribution of Z. That is why the naive threshold z₁₋α′ lets through too many false positives, and why the threshold is taken from the simulated null instead.

With real data, the equivalent of the neutral replicates is an empirical null from genome-wide SNPs, matched on starting frequency and measured in the same samples. It absorbs drift, structure and sample-specific error together, without needing an estimate of Ne.

Model details

Haploid system: weights 1+s, 1 → p* = p(1+s) / (1 + p·s); drift p' = Bin(N(g), p*) / N(g); Ne = N; each sampled individual carries one allele Female zygotes (diploid, haplodiploid): AA = p_f·p_m Aa = p_f(1−p_m) + p_m(1−p_f) aa = (1−p_f)(1−p_m) Selection in females: weights 1+s, 1+hs, 1 → p_f* = (AA(1+s) + ½Aa(1+hs)) / w̄ Males, haplodiploid: allele from the mother only, haploid weights 1+s♂, 1 → p_m* = p_f(1+s♂) / (p_f(1+s♂) + 1 − p_f) Males, diploid: same zygote frequencies as females, weights 1+s♂, 1+hs♂, 1 Male selection: s♂ = s × (male multiplier) Drift (breeding adults): p_f' = Bin(2N_f(g), p_f*) / 2N_f(g) p_m' = Bin(N_m(g), p_m*) / N_m(g) (haplodiploid) or Bin(2N_m(g), p_m*) / 2N_m(g) Generations: g(year) = round((year − first year) / years per generation), G = g(last year) Population change: N(g) = N(first point) × D^(g/G) D = breeding numbers at the last point relative to the first or, with Ne set per interval, breeding numbers scaled so that Ne(g) = Ne of the interval containing g Migration (after drift): p' ← (1 − m)·p' + m·p_source in both sexes Sampled individuals, k = 1: male with probability "males in the sample"; diploid individuals drawn from their sex's post-selection genotype frequencies; haploid males carry one allele, A with probability p_m*, called by majority of reads Sampled families, k > 1: mother drawn from breeding females, father from breeding males; each daughter gets one random maternal and one paternal allele; a haploid son gets one maternal allele only Heterozygosity retained: H(last point) / H(first point) = Π_g (1 − 1 / 2Ne(g))

Selection starts in the generation after the onset year, so a sample at the onset year sits exactly at p₀. Not modelled: population structure, linked selection, random fluctuation in population size, overlapping generations, and mapping reference bias.
